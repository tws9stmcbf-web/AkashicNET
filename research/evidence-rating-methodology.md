# Evidence Rating Methodology

## Purpose

This methodology provides a transparent way to classify research evidence in the Toolkit.
It is intended to support clarity, discourage overstatement, and preserve the distinction between evidence and interpretation.

## Evidence categories

Use these categories consistently across research entries and reference summaries:

- **Established**
  - Evidence is supported by multiple independent sources, replication, and/or systematic review.
  - The finding is consistent with the strongest available evidence in the field.

- **Emerging**
  - Evidence is promising but still requires more replication, larger samples, or stronger methodological confirmation.
  - Findings are plausible and under active investigation.

- **Working hypothesis**
  - The evidence supports a provisional idea, but the evidence base is incomplete or still exploratory.
  - This category is useful for ideas that guide further inquiry.

- **Speculative**
  - Evidence is limited, preliminary, or largely theoretical.
  - Use this label when there is insufficient empirical support to treat the claim as established.

- **Historical / cultural**
  - The source is primarily descriptive of historical, cultural or traditional perspectives.
  - It is valuable for context, but it may not by itself establish scientific evidence.

## Rating criteria

Assess each source using these dimensions:

- Source type and quality
  - Peer-reviewed research, systematic review or meta-analysis carry greater weight than a single case report or anecdote.
  - Primary research is preferred over secondary interpretation.

- Replication and consistency
  - Has the finding been replicated by independent teams?
  - Are results consistent across populations and methods?

- Methodological clarity
  - Is the experimental or observational method clearly described?
  - Were limitations, controls and biases acknowledged?

- Sample size and generalizability
  - Larger, more representative samples generally support stronger evidence.
  - Consider whether the finding applies broadly or only in a narrow context.

- Limitations and uncertainty
  - Document known weaknesses plainly.
  - Avoid language that implies certainty when evidence is tentative.

- Conflicting evidence
  - Note evidence that contradicts or qualifies the finding.
  - Do not ignore alternative interpretations.

## Applying ratings

- Assign one primary `status` label to each entry.
- Include a short rationale for the rating.
- Use the research database framework to store both the rating and the evidence rationale.

Example rationale:

- `status: emerging`
- `evidence_rationale: Evidence comes from a small controlled trial with promising results, but the effect has not yet been replicated in a larger sample.`

## Notes on interpretation

- Ratings describe the current status of the evidence, not a permanent truth.
- Evidence ratings should be updated as new research becomes available.
- A strong rating does not imply that every related claim is true; it implies that this particular source or finding is well supported relative to the available evidence.

## Preserving evidence principles

- Maintain the Toolkit's evidence hierarchy:
  - Evidence → Observation → Interpretation → Hypothesis → Speculation
- Do not conflate evidence rating with endorsement of a claim.
- Use ratings as an aid to transparency, not as an absolute ranking of value.
