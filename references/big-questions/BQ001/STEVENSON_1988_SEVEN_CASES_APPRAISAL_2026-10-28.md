## What this design can establish

This is a selected, retrospective series of investigations using informant interviews and available documents, with follow-up in several cases. It is not a prospective controlled test or representative sample. The authors initially expected four cases to withstand scrutiny; **Shishupal Singh, Kenedi Alkan and David Morris already prompted doubts** (p.2). Thus “initially convincing” below means the feature that drew interest or persuaded informants, not endorsement by every investigator.

The authors use authenticity to concern how faithfully testimony represents events. They distinguish an unlocated previous person from a seriously distorted account, and acknowledge possible accurate elements even in damaged cases (pp.2–3). Authentic testimony would not, by itself, establish reincarnation.

The abstract offers a three/two/one/one grouping: apparent self-deception, apparent village hoaxes, potentially invalid testimony from both families, and a journalist's hoax (p.1). **The narrative does not warrant seven equally certain fraud findings.** This appraisal therefore records each case's actual evidential position.

## Case-by-case appraisal

### 1. Nimal Singh — pp.3–7

**Initial attraction (pp.4–5).** A grieving monk's search seemed to fit an astrological indication; the child's reported references to Sri Lanka and a bespectacled teacher appeared to identify the deceased monk's cousin. The child allegedly selected the deceased's watch over money and claimed it. The informant's reputation and apparently straightforward account encouraged Stevenson to investigate.

**Subsequent checks (pp.5–7).** Pal's 1963 visit elicited villagers' denials that the child had made previous-life statements. Story's 1964 inquiry found that the astrological indications were broad, not exact identifying names. Two witnesses described the watch being handed over first, with an ownership suggestion, rather than a simultaneous choice. The searchers had already explained their purpose to villagers, providing an ordinary information route. Requests for financial help followed.

**Assessment and uncertainty.** The strongest methodological finding is that an apparently evidential recognition test changed character when other witnesses described its administration: it was prompted and uncontrolled. Even a simultaneous watch-versus-cash choice would not uniquely test ownership memory. Grief, flexible matching and reconstructed recollection plausibly explain the monk's conviction; the authors explicitly avoid equating his inaccurate memory with dishonesty (p.7). Story found no proof of deliberate parental fabrication; the authors were more suspicious of villagers' opportunism. This is **supported reconstruction/self-deception with possible opportunistic deception**, not a demonstrated confession or a fully observed causal reconstruction. The mother was not interviewed, and a visit-interval discrepancy remains in n.4 (pp.4–5).

### 2. Wijesuriya — pp.7–10

**Initial attraction (pp.7–9).** A respected judge relayed a story of a poor child identifying as a murdered wealthy landowner, recognizing the landowner's clergyman son despite a disguise, and bearing a matching chest mark. The story also fitted an attractive religious and moral narrative.

**Subsequent checks (pp.8–10).** The judge was a secondhand source. The monk who supplied the story could not establish the child's name and address from his clinic records; investigators' searches in the reported locality also failed. The autopsy did not support the wound location remembered by that monk, although his memory of the child's mark could itself be wrong. The clergyman denied the alleged encounter and recognition.

**Assessment and uncertainty.** The celebrated recognition is **contradicted by its supposed participant** and was judged false by the authors. The child remained **untraced**, not shown never to have existed. The paper expressly leaves alternatives: wrong locality, a village hoax later suppressed, suppression of a genuine report, or an insufficient search (pp.9–10). Hoax is therefore a hypothesis, not a demonstrated explanation of the whole story. Respectability, narrative appeal and presumed doctrinal hostility cannot substitute for locating witnesses and records. The authors' favourable impression of the clergyman is also a credibility judgment, not an independently controlled finding.

### 3. Shishupal Singh — pp.10–15

**Initial attraction (pp.10–13).** Public claims of being Gandhi reborn, a chest mark, reported recognitions and continuing family/village endorsement sustained interest. Investigators nevertheless knew of a critical newspaper report before their visits. Stevenson interviewed relatives in 1971; Pasricha interviewed Shishupal in 1983.

**Subsequent checks (pp.11–14).** A teacher reported many wrong answers and covert reading about Gandhi, later reaffirming that account in correspondence and interview. He also alleged coaching by neighbours seeking possible benefits. One alleged recognition witness denied ever meeting Shishupal. Another visitor reported a correct town identification alongside vague answers. Father's and son's developmental chronologies differed; Shishupal denied reading Gandhi books and continued to believe the claim.

**Assessment and uncertainty.** The authors judged **fraudulent elements supported**, chiefly by the reported concealed study and inflated recognition testimony, while allowing a hypothetical mixture of early experiences, suggestion and later embellishment (p.14). That hypothetical early-memory sequence is not established history. Financial/publicity motives and an apparent attempt to avoid an interview do not prove fraud on their own. The teacher's roughly 80% wrong-answer estimate is not a reproducible accuracy rate: no complete scored inventory is supplied. The authors explicitly reject inability to answer questions as a sufficient fraud criterion (pp.14–15). The later-observed chest mark was reasonably close to two reported wounds, but not securely established as congenital (p.14, with birth-date uncertainty in p.10 n.5). **Suspected coaching and supported ordinary acquisition are not proof that every experience or utterance was deliberately fabricated.**

### 4. Mahender Jain — pp.15–18

**Initial attraction (pp.16–17).** Newspapers reported correspondence before the two families met, making this a priority for investigators seeking early written statements. A drowned child's father saw affectionate behaviour, resemblance and a correct response about a ball found with his son's body.

**Subsequent checks (pp.15–18).** The mother supplied lists from which Mahender selected personal and place names; these were not spontaneous proper-name productions. She knew of the proposed family, and her sister had previously negotiated adoption with the bereaved father. The investigators examined three letters; the first approach concerned adoption and economic distress. There were mismatches in residence, maternal form of address and sibling history. The claim of being pushed into a well was not verified; the deceased child's manner of entering the well was uncertain. The ball answer followed the father's question during contact.

**Assessment and uncertainty.** **Prompted target selection and a concrete indirect information pathway are documented in the authors' account.** Overhearing specific conversations is proposed, not observed. Ordinary learning, broad statements, selective matching, grief and adoption interests offer substantial alternatives without requiring conscious fraud. The father did not immediately accept the identification; by 1985 adoption had been abandoned and the parents' beliefs differed. The authors leave open some genuine-memory possibility but find almost no evidence for this particular identification (p.18). **Premeeting letters do not establish pre-exposure, unprompted or independently selected information.**

### 5. Mahesh Shakya — pp.18–22

**Initial attraction (pp.19–20).** Early-childhood references to a murdered Brahmin teacher, apparently appropriate behaviour and an apparently typical developmental course led Pasricha to expect confirmation from the proposed previous family.

**Subsequent checks (pp.19–21).** The families lived nearby; Mahesh's father knew the deceased, owed him money and had seen his body. The murder was public knowledge. The proposed previous family denied convincing private knowledge and recognitions. One relative reported that an uncle obtained a coaching confession, but **that uncle did not corroborate the confession in his own interview** (p.20). The alleged debt repayment remained undocumented and disputed after repeated interviews.

**Assessment and uncertainty.** This is **unresolved conflicting testimony with possible bias on both sides**, not demonstrated fraud. Debt avoidance could motivate the child's family; social and caste prejudice could motivate rejection by the other family. Neither motive establishes what happened. The authors explicitly withhold a definite interpretation (p.21), then express their belief that Mahesh probably had some real memories, possibly of the proposed person, while allowing premature misidentification (p.22). That belief must be recorded faithfully **as their interpretation**, not accepted verification. Neither the alleged confession nor the authors' favourable conjectures resolve the contradiction. The case particularly requires independent evidence rather than choosing the witness group most congenial to a preferred conclusion.

### 6. Kenedi Alkan — pp.22–26

**Initial attraction (pp.23–24).** A father's dream before birth, naming after John F. Kennedy, later self-identification, unusual traits and eventually a purported wound-corresponding mark sustained family belief. Stevenson was doubtful from the outset.

**Subsequent checks (pp.23–25).** The 1967 recorded interview, when Kenedi was two, contained only the reported presidential self-identification and an explicit report of no birthmark. Polat's 1985 follow-up, undertaken without knowing of the earlier inquiry, elicited a richer narrative, a different onset age, and a claimed shoulder mark. The paper reports that the mark did not correspond to Kennedy's wounds. The biographical details were general public information.

**Assessment and uncertainty.** **Parental identification, ordinary learning and reinforcement are well-supported explanatory candidates**, strengthened by the earlier record's contrast with later recollection. The exact transmission sequence is reconstructed, not experimentally observed; the onset and mark discrepancy does not itself establish deliberate lying. Polat's initially separate inquiry is a useful follow-up, not replication of anomalous acquisition. The authors also leave open whether distinctive traits preceded the identification or reflected differential attention (p.26). Persistence of sincere belief, family affection and unusual behaviour do not independently validate the identity.

### 7. David Morris — pp.26–29

**Initial attraction (p.27).** A *Fate* story alleged ancient Hebrew, a temple model, authoritative endorsements and discovery of an archaeological passage. These impressed readers; Stevenson already doubted the report.

**Subsequent checks (pp.28–29).** Two inquiries, one by Berendt and one commissioned by Edi-Monde, reportedly failed to locate the dentist-father and several named authorities in relevant professional/institutional records. A real scholar had the wrong discipline and knew nothing of the case. The photograph's uniform was inconsistent with the purported date; the allegedly newly discovered passage was already known. The paper reports *Fate*'s retraction and cancellation of further articles by the same author. The writer later invoked pseudonyms, although the original story had not disclosed that qualification.

**Assessment and uncertainty.** This is the paper's **strongest documented exposure of fabricated reporting**, supported by converging checks and a reported retraction, rather than merely weak performance by a child. The 1988 paper is a retrospective account of those inquiries; this appraisal has not freshly authenticated the original reports or retraction. Nor does it independently prove that no person corresponding to any pseudonym ever existed. The justified disposition is **journalistic hoax as reported and supported in the paper**, not a general conclusion about other child cases.

## Methodological contribution and limits

The series usefully reports disappointing investigations, preserves disagreements, revisits some cases and sometimes checks documents or witnesses against an attractive first account. Nimal shows how an apparently strong test can acquire its strength through retelling; Mahender shows why “before meeting” and “before exposure” require separate dates; Kenedi shows why early recordings can constrain later narratives. Mahesh shows that the supposedly disconfirming witnesses also require scrutiny.

Its limitations constrain those lessons:

1. **Selection and missing denominators.** Cases were selected for investigation and for sufficiently confident reporting; unresolved surveillance cases and some insubstantial cases were excluded or dropped (pp.2–3). No complete intake-to-disposition ledger, detection-sensitivity estimate or blinded case audit is supplied. Retained country totals on p.3 are not a denominator for estimating deception prevalence. Neither “seven exposed” nor the much larger retained collection yields a defensible fraud rate or probability that remaining cases are genuine.
2. **Retrospective evidence and dependence.** Several inquiries occurred long after childhood events. Summaries organize interviews and reproduce selected reports, not a complete independently coded corpus. Multiple relatives, publications or retellings may share one source; their agreement is not automatically independent corroboration.
3. **Uneven controls.** Useful checks are present, but no uniform prospective protocol, blinded alternative matching, complete statement/miss ledger, preregistered scoring or comparable control population is reported. The series illustrates failure mechanisms; it does not measure how often they operate or demonstrate that every unexplained detail is explained.
4. **Inference about intent.** Contradictions, ordinary knowledge, potential benefits and witness motives weaken evidential claims but do not each prove intentional deception. “Self-deception” here is an explanatory interpretation, not a clinical diagnosis. The authors' residual paranormal possibilities are also interpretations.
5. **Symmetric scrutiny and cultural care.** Professional standing and apparent candour are fallible credibility cues. Religious identity, poverty, bereavement and caste position cannot be used as truth or dishonesty tests. The paper's caste and class characterizations should be contextualized critically, not adopted as judgments about participants or communities. No suffering is treated as deserved.

These cases weaken reliance on particular testimony and matching procedures. They neither refute every reincarnation report nor establish survival because some residual questions remain.

## Proposed contribution for HIERATIC017

**Editorial candidate only; independent and human review required before use.** HIERATIC017's 1 November 2026 date remains provisional; no article or website is updated here.

Stevenson, Pasricha and Samararatne's 1988 paper shows why apparently persuasive reincarnation reports require checks beyond a compelling story. Across seven investigations, the authors describe prompted recognition, prior information routes, reconstructed testimony, disputed claims and a reported journalistic hoax. Their conclusions differ in certainty: one child remained untraced, and another case's interpretation was explicitly undecided. The paper therefore supports neither “seven proven frauds” nor a deception rate for the wider literature. Its methodological lesson also applies to apparently supportive reports: preserve statements before matching, distinguish that date from first exposure, document questioning, check both families independently and retain wrong or ambiguous details. Genuine testimony, accurate correspondence, unexplained information and personal continuity are separate questions. This appraisal leaves BQ001 unresolved.

**Suggested citation:** Stevenson et al. (1988), pp.2–3, 5–10, 15–22, 28–30. Detailed case locators above support the compressed passage.
