# Citizen Science Protocol: Community Signal to Testable Research

## Purpose

This protocol provides a reusable template for turning observations, community signals, and exploratory questions into testable research within the Noetic Sciences Toolkit.

It is designed to preserve the distinction between:

- community observation
- candidate hypothesis
- formal protocol
- measured data
- evidence update

The protocol is intentionally conservative. It supports iterative learning without claiming that informal or community-based reports are equivalent to empirical evidence.

This document follows the repository's Phase 2 standards for evidence rating, research database structure, and transparent documentation.

## Pathway

Community Signal
→ Observation
→ Question
→ Hypothesis
→ Protocol
→ Data
→ Analysis
→ Replication
→ Evidence Update

## 1. Research Question

State the specific question to be investigated in plain language and in operational terms.

Examples of acceptable framing:

- What patterns appear when people report a recurring experience under a defined condition?
- Does a defined intervention or context show a measurable change in a selected outcome?
- Can a conceptual proposition be translated into a testable and observable prediction?

The question should be:

- clear
- bounded
- answerable with the planned method
- distinguishable from speculation or belief

## 2. Background and Rationale

Describe the context and why the question is worth studying.

Include:

- salient observations that prompted the inquiry
- relevant historical or cultural context, if applicable
- any known literature or internal project materials relevant to the conceptual field
- why this question matters to the Toolkit
- the specific gaps the protocol seeks to explore

Community sources such as r/NeuronsToNirvana and r/TribalGathering may be used to generate candidate questions or recurring themes. They should be recorded as potential hypothesis-generation inputs and clearly distinguished from evidence.

The same caution applies to conceptual frameworks such as HIERATIC and HOMESENSE. These may suggest propositions worth operationalising, but they are not treated as scientifically validated theories by default.

## 3. Hypothesis

State one primary hypothesis and, if needed, secondary hypotheses.

A good hypothesis should be:

- specific
- falsifiable or testable
- aligned with the study design
- stated as a prediction, not an assumption disguised as fact

Example format:

- Primary hypothesis: Under [condition], [group] will show a measurable difference in [outcome] compared with [baseline or comparator].
- Secondary hypothesis: The relationship will be stronger for [subgroup] than for [other subgroup].

If the study is exploratory, identify the hypothesis as provisional and note that it is designed to guide pattern detection, not to establish causal certainty.

## 4. Exploratory vs Confirmatory Status

Classify the protocol explicitly.

Possible status labels:

- Exploratory: designed to generate hypotheses, examine patterns, or refine measures
- Confirmatory: designed to test a pre-specified hypothesis using defined criteria
- Mixed methods: exploratory and confirmatory elements combined in a single design
- N-of-1 observational: a single participant or system used to study change over time

The status should determine how strongly the study can support interpretation.

Important principle:

- community observations and exploratory work can generate questions
- they are not equivalent to population-level evidence or causal proof

## 5. Preregistration

Document whether the protocol is preregistered before data collection begins.

If preregistered, include:

- platform or repository used
- date of preregistration
- research question
- primary hypothesis
- primary outcome
- analysis plan
- stopping or exclusion rules, if applicable

If not preregistered, state that it is exploratory and that findings should be interpreted cautiously.

This protocol does not require a claim of certainty. It requires explicit documentation of the plan and the basis for interpretation.

## 6. Study Design

Describe the overall design, including:

- observational, comparative, repeated-measures, cross-sectional, longitudinal, mixed methods, or naturalistic design
- whether the study is within-subject or between-subject
- the time frame for data collection
- whether an intervention, prompt, or structured process is involved
- how the design supports answerability of the research question

The design should match the question and the evidential ambition of the project.

## 7. Participants / Sampling

Specify:

- target population
- inclusion criteria
- exclusion criteria
- recruitment method
- sample size rationale, if any
- whether sampling is purposive, convenience, self-selected, or community-based
- demographic, contextual, or experiential characteristics relevant to interpretation

For citizen-science work, record whether participants are community volunteers, contributors, or invited participants.

Sampling choices must be documented transparently, and participant selection should not be hidden behind vague or informal claims.

## 8. Variables

List all variables clearly.

Include:

- independent variables
- dependent variables
- moderators or effect modifiers
- covariates or confounders
- time-varying factors

Examples:

- independent variable: context, intervention, time, task condition, environmental exposure
- dependent variable: self-reported experience, behaviour, mood, performance, physiological signal, or qualitative outcome

## 9. Outcomes

Define all outcomes before analysis where possible.

Include:

- primary outcome(s)
- secondary outcome(s)
- exploratory outcome(s)
- any qualitative or interpretive outcomes

State whether the outcome is:

- subjective
- behavioural
- physiological
- qualitative
- performance-based

The outcome definition must be precise enough that another researcher can understand what was measured and how.

## 10. Measurements

Describe the tools and procedures used to collect data.

Include:

- instruments or questionnaires
- measurement schedule
- timing and frequency
- method of administration
- instructions provided to participants
- training or calibration procedures
- whether the measure is validated or exploratory

If a measure is novel, informal, or not validated, say so explicitly and note its exploratory status.

## 11. Controls and Confounders

Document the most likely confounding influences and the strategies used to address them.

Examples of confounders:

- expectation effects
- prior beliefs
- sleep quality
- context shifts
- social influence
- mode of delivery
- sample selection bias
- environmental conditions
- measurement timing

Controls may include:

- baseline measures
- repeated measures
- comparison conditions
- standardised instructions
- limited time windows
- blinding if feasible
- structured documentation of context

Where full control is not possible, state clearly what uncertainty remains.

## 12. Data Collection

Describe the procedure for obtaining, storing, and organising the data.

Include:

- method of collection
- data entry format
- software or platform used
- raw record handling
- data storage location or pathway
- file naming conventions
- whether data are anonymous, pseudonymised, or identifiable

Data collection should be transparent and traceable. The protocol should produce a record that another researcher could inspect and reproduce without requiring undocumented assumptions.

## 13. Analysis Plan

Describe how the data will be analysed.

For exploratory work:

- summarise patterns
- compare observations across time or conditions
- identify anomalies or recurring themes
- report uncertainty explicitly

For confirmatory work:

- specify the test or statistical method in advance
- define decision rules for support or non-support
- state how multiple comparisons or subgroup analyses will be handled

The analysis plan should be stated before data are examined in a way that supports the intended level of inference.

## 14. Missing Data

Describe how missing or incomplete data will be handled.

Options include:

- omission with transparency
- sensitivity checks
- summarisation by completeness
- planned follow-up for missing data
- exclusion rules for invalid or unusable observations

The protocol should not silently ignore gaps. Missing data should be reported clearly and factored into interpretation.

## 15. Reproducibility

State how the protocol can be reproduced, reviewed, or extended.

Include:

- description of procedures in sufficient detail
- timing, instructions, and participant flow
- measurement details and instruments
- analysis workflow
- any code, notebooks, templates, or scripts used

Reproducibility does not require perfect replication, but it does require enough documentation for another researcher to understand what was done and how decisions were made.

## 16. Ethics and Informed Consent

Every protocol should articulate ethical safeguards.

Include:

- consent process
- voluntary participation
- right to withdraw
- any potential risks or burdens
- whether the research is minimal-risk or requires additional oversight
- whether professional or supervisory review is appropriate

This protocol should not be used to bypass normal ethical review or responsible practice. For any participant-facing research, appropriate consent and oversight are required.

## 17. Privacy and Data Minimisation

Protect participant privacy throughout the process.

Rules:

- collect only the minimum data necessary
- avoid unnecessary personal identifiers
- prefer pseudonymised or anonymised records where feasible
- store sensitive material securely
- do not scrape or expose personal data from public communities
- do not publish identifiable observations without explicit consent and appropriate safeguards

Community signals can be aggregated or summarised without exposing personal content. The protocol should favour de-identified and minimal records when possible.

## 18. Safety

Document possible safety risks and how they will be managed.

Examples:

- distress from reflection or disclosure
- fatigue or cognitive overload
- inappropriate or unreviewed health claims
- adverse reactions to interventions
- emotional discomfort or social harm

Where relevant, include emergency procedures, referral pathways, or limits on participant obligations. The project does not replace medical, legal, psychological, or professional advice as appropriate to the context.

## 19. Limitations

Identify the protocol's known limitations honestly.

Examples:

- small or self-selected samples
- ambiguous measures
- observational design without causal control
- reliance on self-report
- confounding variables
- inability to generalise beyond the study context
- weak signal-to-noise ratio in community-sourced observations

A well-designed protocol should clearly state what it cannot establish.

## 20. Interpretation

Interpret the results using the Toolkit's evidence hierarchy:

Evidence → Observation → Interpretation → Hypothesis → Speculation

Use careful language:

- observed trend
- recurring pattern
- possible association
- provisional support
- inconclusive
- not established

Community observations may be useful for generating hypotheses, but they should not be interpreted as direct evidence of causality or validated theory.

The interpretation should separate:

- what was observed
- what was inferred
- what remains speculative
- what requires replication

## 21. Replication and Follow-up

Outline how the study may be repeated or extended.

Include:

- replication opportunities
- follow-up questions
- changes to design if the first iteration is inconclusive
- criteria for a stronger second study
- whether the protocol should become confirmatory after exploratory learning

Replication is essential for moving from preliminary patterns toward meaningful evidence. A useful early study is not automatically a complete or decisive study.

## 22. Evidence Update

After analysis, classify the result according to Phase 2 evidence standards.

Options include:

- Established
- Emerging
- Working hypothesis
- Speculative
- Historical / Cultural

The status should reflect the evidence actually generated and should not be inflated by enthusiasm, novelty, or conceptual appeal.

Any journal, database, or research record should reflect this status explicitly and link back to the underlying protocol, data, and limitations.

## 23. Documentation Checklist

Before a protocol is considered usable, the following should be recorded:

- research question
- background and rationale
- hypothesis
- exploratory/confirmatory status
- preregistration status
- design
- sample
- variables and outcomes
- measurements
- controls and confounders
- data collection method
- analysis plan
- missing data approach
- reproducibility notes
- ethics and consent details
- privacy and minimisation notes
- safety notes
- limitations
- interpretation plan
- replication and follow-up plan
- evidence rating

## 24. Working Principles

This protocol is designed to support disciplined inquiry without imposing a single worldview.

It is built around these core principles:

- observations are not automatically evidence
- community signals can generate hypotheses
- conceptual frameworks can guide operationalisation
- evidence must be separated from interpretation
- strong claims require stronger methods
- replication matters
- transparency is part of the method

This protocol is a reusable foundation for the Toolkit's Phase 3 experimental work and should be adapted to the needs of each study rather than treated as a fixed doctrine.
