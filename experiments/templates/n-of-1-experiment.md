# N-of-1 / Citizen-Science Experiment Template

## 1. Study title

[Insert a concise working title for the study.]

## 2. Research question

What specific question is being explored?

Example format:

- Does a particular routine, exposure, or context appear to change a defined experience, behaviour, or measurement in this participant over time?
- Under what conditions does a selected outcome become more likely to increase, decrease, or vary?

## 3. Background

Describe the context, prior observations, and rationale for the study.

Include:

- the motivating observation or question
- relevant conceptual frameworks or project themes
- any known concerns or prior experience patterns
- how this study connects to the wider Toolkit research process

This template is designed to support structured inquiry, not to assume that community observations or conceptual propositions are already established evidence.

Community sources such as r/NeuronsToNirvana and r/TribalGathering may help generate candidates for observation or hypothesis generation. They are explicitly not empirical evidence and should not be treated as proof of effect.

Conceptual frameworks such as HIERATIC and HOMESENSE may suggest operational hypotheses that can be tested, but they are not treated as scientifically validated theories within this template.

## 4. Hypothesis

State the tentative prediction being tested.

Example format:

- Primary hypothesis: The selected intervention or condition will be associated with a measurable change in [outcome] compared with [baseline or comparison condition].
- Secondary hypothesis: The direction or magnitude of the change will vary depending on [context, timing, or participant state].

Keep the hypothesis clearly provisional if the study is exploratory.

## 5. Exploratory vs confirmatory status

Indicate which applies:

- Exploratory: designed to generate hypotheses, identify patterns, refine measures, or examine whether a phenomenon is worth testing more rigorously.
- Confirmatory: designed to test a pre-specified hypothesis with defined criteria and a structured analysis plan.
- Mixed: includes both exploratory and confirmatory elements.

Important distinction:

- a single participant study can be highly informative for pattern detection
- it does not by itself establish generalisable causal conclusions

## 6. Preregistration

Document whether the study was preregistered or whether it remains exploratory.

Include:

- preregistration status: preregistered / not preregistered / planned
- date if applicable
- study question
- primary outcome
- analysis plan
- any criteria for excluding data or stopping the study

If not preregistered, note that the study should be interpreted as exploratory and provisional.

## 7. Study design

Describe the design in a few sentences.

Examples:

- repeated-measures N-of-1 design
- baseline-follow-up design
- alternating condition design
- naturalistic observation with structured scoring
- mixed methods observation + self-report

Specify the time window, number of cycles, and whether the design is within-subject or between-subject.

## 8. Participant / N-of-1 description

Describe the participant or single-case unit.

Include:

- participant role or relationship to the study
- relevant background only if necessary and ethically appropriate
- inclusion and exclusion considerations
- context relevant to the phenomenon under study
- whether the participant is self-selected or recruited through a structured process

Do not collect or record personal data beyond what is necessary for the study.

## 9. Intervention or exposure

Describe the intervention, exposure, routine, context, or condition being studied.

Include:

- exact protocol or procedure
- duration and frequency
- timing and sequence
- any instructions or materials
- whether the intervention is standardised or adapted to the participant

If no intervention is being tested, state clearly what is being observed instead.

## 10. Control / comparison condition

Describe the comparison or control condition.

Examples:

- baseline period
- usual routine
- alternative condition
- sham or neutral condition
- repeated comparison across time

If no comparison condition is feasible, explain the limitation and why the design remains useful.

## 11. Variables

List the variables clearly.

Examples:

- independent variable: condition, timing, exposure, setting, activity
- dependent variable: experience rating, behavioural measure, mood, performance metric, physiological signal, qualitative description
- confounders: sleep, stress, environment, prior practice, social context, expectation

Keep variables operational and easy to interpret.

## 12. Measurements

Describe how the outcome is measured.

Include:

- measures used
- instruments or questionnaires
- timing of measurement
- rating scales or thresholds
- whether the measure is validated, self-report, observational, or exploratory

If a measure is not established, identify it as exploratory.

## 13. Outcomes

Define the primary, secondary, and exploratory outcomes.

For example:

- Primary outcome: change in a target experience or behavioural metric
- Secondary outcome: change in mood, engagement, or quality of experience
- Exploratory outcome: pattern detection or qualitative theme

Each outcome should be described in measurable terms and linked to the hypothesis.

## 14. Data collection schedule

Document the schedule for observation and measurement.

Examples:

- Daily baseline rating for 7 days
- Condition A for 3 days, then condition B for 3 days
- Pre/post measurement before and after exposure
- Structured note entry after each session

Include the frequency, duration, and order of recording.

## 15. Confounders

List the most likely confounders and how they will be accounted for.

Potential confounders may include:

- sleep quality
- caffeine or alcohol use
- stress or mood state
- social interaction
- environmental conditions
- expectation or prior beliefs
- external events or schedule changes

State whether the design controls for them directly, measures them, or acknowledges them as uncontrolled limits.

## 16. Adverse events / safety

Describe known risks and safety procedures.

Include:

- possible discomfort or adverse effect
- how adverse events will be monitored
- thresholds for stopping or pausing the study
- whether qualified supervision is needed
- what actions will be taken if the participant is distressed or unwell

This scaffold does not replace appropriate professional advice where relevant.

## 17. Analysis plan

Describe how the results will be evaluated.

Examples:

- visual inspection of repeated measures
- summary of within-person change across time
- comparison between condition and baseline
- thematic review of qualitative entries
- descriptive statistics if appropriate

For exploratory designs, emphasise pattern-building and question generation. For confirmatory work, define exact decision rules in advance.

## 18. Missing data

Describe what will happen if data are incomplete or missing.

Include:

- how missing points are handled
- how data quality problems are documented
- whether missing data are excluded, noted, or summarised
- any plan for follow-up or re-measurement

Missing data should be reported transparently and not hidden.

## 19. Results

Record the observed results in a neutral, factual style.

Include:

- summary of the data collected
- pattern of change over time
- differences between conditions if applicable
- note whether the measured outcomes changed in the expected direction
- binary or qualitative description of the pattern observed

This section should not overstate what the data do or do not show.

## 20. Interpretation

Explain how the results should be interpreted in light of the evidence hierarchy.

Use language such as:

- tentative pattern
- exploratory signal
- possible association
- unclear or inconclusive
- not established

The interpretation should distinguish between:

- what was observed
- what was inferred
- what remains speculative

## 21. Limitations

State the study's known limitations.

Potential limitations include:

- single-case design
- limited external validity
- self-selection or convenience participation
- expectation effects
- low sample size
- measurement error
- uncontrolled confounding
- difficulty generalising beyond the immediate context

Acknowledge that N-of-1 findings can generate hypotheses without establishing broad causal claims.

## 22. Replication plan

Describe how the study could be repeated or extended.

Examples:

- repeat the protocol in a second participant or under a second time window
- test the same question with a more standardised design
- refine the intervention based on initial findings
- convert an exploratory pattern into a more structured confirmatory protocol

Replication is essential for moving from interesting observations toward stronger evidence.

## 23. Evidence rating

Assign a status using the repository's evidence-rating methodology.

Possible labels:

- Established
- Emerging
- Working hypothesis
- Speculative
- Historical / Cultural

The rating should reflect the evidence actually generated and should not be inflated by novelty or plausibility.

## 24. References

Provide references or project links relevant to the project context.

Use the repository reference format from [research/REFERENCE_FORMAT.md](../../references/REFERENCE_FORMAT.md) and link to relevant material in the research database where appropriate.

Examples of relevant references may include:

- internal research entries
- project framework documents
- methods notes
- community signal workflow documentation
- project-specific protocol notes

Do not invent citations.

- whether the record is a draft, revision, or finalised case record

## EXAMPLE ONLY

This section is an example for template use only. It is not evidence, not a real finding, and not a scientific claim.

### Example title

Tracking a daily wellbeing routine in a single participant

### Example research question

Does a structured morning routine appear to correlate with changes in self-rated alertness or mood over a two-week period?

### Example background

The study is a template example only. It is meant to show how a question might be framed and operationalised in a single-case design. It does not represent any validated result.

### Example hypothesis

A structured routine will be associated with improved self-rated alertness during the study period compared with a baseline period.

### Example status

Exploratory, single-case, hypothesis-generating framework.

### Example methods

A participant records morning alertness and mood daily using a simple scale for 14 days, while noting routines, sleep quality, and contextual factors.

### Example interpretation

The example would only be interpreted as a pattern worth testing further. It would not be treated as proof of an effect.
